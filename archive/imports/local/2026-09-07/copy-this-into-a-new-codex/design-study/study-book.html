Processes, traditions and internet aesthetic labels

Processes, traditions and internet aesthetic labels

Research contributor: brand/history review agent. Checked 7 September 2026. Educational study only; no Levarum direction is selected here.

Evidence and scope

These five names do not describe equivalent things. Risograph names a printing technology; Gzhel names a regional ceramic tradition with changing forms; Austurbane is an attributed contemporary classification; Clean Girl Brutalism remains an unverified compound label; Asian Chic has documented usage in fashion scholarship, but that does not establish the provenance of a similarly labelled graphic-design screenshot.

I inspected original screenshots 01 (Risograph), 05 (Austurbane), 06 (Clean Girl Brutalism), 08 (Gzhel) and 09 (overview) after their files became available. The overview is the only provided view of Asian Chic. No creator is identified from a blurred credit. “Lessons” and “misuses” are design interpretations rather than experimentally established outcomes.

What the supplied examples actually show

  • 01: A white-ground exhibition poster uses enormous, separated magenta letters around overlapping cyan/yellow/magenta animal imagery. The visual mechanism is translucent layering plus a hierarchy that remains large enough to compete with illustration. A screenshot cannot verify its physical printing process.
  • 05: Three pastel blue, sage and cream rectangular packages overlap at different angles. Fine, high-contrast serif titles occupy their top edges; thin geometric marks sit centrally. This particular example teaches angle, overlap and restraint more directly than CARI's description of dramatic natural-material photography. The small brand/creator text is not verified.
  • 06: The white AGNI package concentrates visual weight in a large black uppercase name at the top and a small saturated pink information block near the bottom. A fine vertical rule and rotated information strengthen the edge. The mechanism is extreme scale contrast and asymmetrical information placement, not concrete texture or visual damage.
  • 08: A LOEWE shopping bag contains a tile-like grid of blue birds and foliage beneath a large black name. This is verified visual content; the slide's “Gzhel” label is not verified provenance. The De Morgan Foundation documents LOEWE's 2019 collection inspired by William De Morgan, including bird motifs and blue colorways. That is a plausible alternative research lead, not a confirmed match to this bag. [H13]
  • 09, Asian Chic thumbnail: A densely framed portrait uses red, yellow and turquoise with large border lettering. Resolution is insufficient to establish maker, script or cultural lineage. The observable lesson is ornamental framing and saturated contrast; assigning a unified continental style would add information the image does not establish.

Risograph: understand the printing logic before imitating the texture

RISO's own history distinguishes the 1958 RISO-GRAPH mimeograph device from the AP7200/FX7200 generation listed under 1980. Saying simply that “Risograph was invented in 1980” loses that precursor. The Japanese manufacturer is the primary source for this chronology; its page cautions that listed release years apply to Japan. [H1]

Working print studio PageMasters describes digital stencil duplication with spot inks: images are separated into grayscale files, a master is formed and wrapped around a drum, and tones become halftones. Its current two-drum equipment can print two colors in a pass. Transparent nonmetallic inks permit overlapping layers to produce further colors. Registration can shift, and trapping deliberately overlaps adjacent shapes to reduce visible gaps. These are production relationships, not a universal requirement that every print look damaged. [H2]

RISOTTO's preparation guidance adds an important corrective to the romantic language of “imperfection”: operators actively manage excessive coverage, feed marks and fine detail. Their suggested settings are studio-specific production guidance, not web-design rules. [H3]

Lesson 1: Build an illustration from two independently intelligible color layers, then inspect their intersection as a third relationship. The palette becomes structural: overlap can mean connection, combination or comparison rather than merely decoration.

Lesson 2: Separate expressive image texture from informational precision. A grainy image can coexist with crisp text. Test the design in flat separated colors before adding grain; if it stops working, texture was masking a compositional weakness.

Common misuses: Adding random noise to an ordinary RGB gradient and calling it authentic Riso; exaggerating registration errors across small body text until a readable printing method becomes a legibility gimmick. A digital imitation should be described as Riso-inspired, not as a physical print or proof of sustainable production.

Gzhel: a living regional craft, not a synonym for blue decoration

The familiar cobalt-on-white appearance is only part of Gzhel's history. The Russian Federation's Commission for UNESCO describes an older pottery region and a twentieth-century revival involving Alexander Saltykov and Natalia Bessarabova. Its account associates the modern identity with underglaze cobalt, tonal brushwork and teaching a vocabulary of strokes. The institution is a national commission, not evidence of a UNESCO heritage designation. [H4]

The All-Russian Decorative Art Museum's search-indexed exhibition description traces eighteenth-century multicolor majolica, nineteenth-century ceramics and later revival. Direct opening returned 404, so this is corroborating discovery evidence rather than a fully inspected museum page. [H5] Avoid assigning one exact origin date to every material, style and workshop under the Gzhel name.

The Gzhel Porcelain Factory documents a collaboration with Sergey Sysoev beginning in 2018: its artists hand-apply cobalt decoration, including enlarged motifs, with gold additions. This primary contemporary example establishes that the practice can be reinterpreted by named collaborators; tradition does not require endlessly repeating one dense floral surface. The factory's sales and recognition claims are promotional and are not independently verified here. [H6]

Lesson 1: Study a motif's construction, not just its silhouette. A broad stroke with internal tonal variation can produce volume while a fine stem creates direction. One hue can carry multiple visual roles through density, scale and edge quality.

Lesson 2: Treat unpainted space as an active part of the pattern. Compare a dense border with a single enlarged motif; the same vocabulary can produce ceremonial abundance or restraint through placement.

Common misuses: Calling any blue-and-white floral graphic Gzhel without evidence of its source; flattening a changing regional craft into a timeless generic “Slavic” ornamental asset. Identify whether an example is historical pottery, contemporary work by a named maker, or a digital interpretation. Do not confuse it with Delftware or Chinese blue-and-white solely because colors overlap.

Austurbane: a useful attributed label, not an ancient school

CARI explicitly credits Alex Edwards with both foundational research and the name, explaining it as a combination of austere and urbane. Its classification emphasizes restrained color, serifs, generous empty space, natural materials and staged product photography with dramatic illumination and refractive or botanical shadows. CARI places relevance from the late 2010s onward; that is the institute's classification, not a independently measured universal timeline. [H7]

CARI describes itself as a collective of researchers and designers categorizing consumer aesthetics. This makes its archive a primary source for its own terminology, rather than proof that every designer shown intended to join a movement. [H8]

Lesson 1: Make lighting perform compositional work. A cast shadow can link objects, provide directional tension or frame a subject without adding another graphic component.

Lesson 2: Evaluate material contrast independently of hue: rough stone against smooth glass, translucent against opaque, soft fabric against a precise edge. Photograph the same arrangement under diffuse and hard light to understand how atmosphere changes.

Common misuses: Equating beige plus a serif with sophistication regardless of subject; copying familiar pedestal/branch-shadow arrangements so closely that a supposedly refined identity becomes interchangeable. Neither a serif nor a neutral palette is intrinsically expensive-looking or effective.

Clean Girl Brutalism: retain the label, disclose the uncertainty

Exact and variant searches did not locate an authoritative originator or stable definition of this compound. That is a retrieval limitation, not proof that nobody uses it. Treat it as a supplied descriptive label pending the original creator's explanation.

Two related references help avoid invented history. RIBA describes architectural Brutalism through exposed materials, construction, texture and expressive mass, discussing the Smithsons and mid-century buildings. CARI's separate Nu-Brutalism category describes graphic typographic structure, contrasting colors and grotesque type. Neither source verifies “Clean Girl Brutalism” as their category. [H9–H10]

Lesson 1: Analyze a proposed hybrid by oppositions: soft image versus hard grid; generous space versus heavy type; restrained palette versus abrupt scale. Name the actual tension instead of relying on the trend name to explain it.

Lesson 2: Use a single-variable exercise: keep photography and content fixed while changing only type weight and boundary lines. This reveals whether the supposed “brutalism” contributes hierarchy or just styling.

Common misuses: Treating harshness or deliberate interface friction as architectural authenticity; using “clean” to justify erasing useful labels, controls or contrast. A sparse screenshot is not evidence of a functional interface.

Asian Chic: documented phrase, unstable umbrella

Ann Marie Leshkowich and Carla Jones's 2003 article examines Asian Chic through fieldwork in Vietnam and Indonesia. Their analysis concerns fashion, identity, authorship and audiences; the meaning of a borrowed element changes with who produces and interprets it. This is evidence of prior scholarly usage, not attribution of the user's graphic label or a recipe for a single Asian visual language. The PDF is hosted by the University of Colorado. [H11]

The V&A's account of chinoiserie provides a concrete historical comparison: European designers combined and imagined motifs from different cultures rather than reliably representing them. This is a distinct historical subject, not a synonym for contemporary Asian design. [H12]

Lesson 1: Replace the umbrella with an attributable object: a named poster, ceramic, publication or textile, with place, maker and date when known. Then analyze its lettering, rhythm, material and composition.

Lesson 2: Separate mechanism from cultural sign. Layering, asymmetry and generous space can be studied without importing a sacred symbol or pretending to write another language. If lettering is essential, understand its meaning and involve someone competent in that script.

Common misuses: Mixing unrelated scripts and motifs to signal undifferentiated exoticism; labelling contemporary work “traditional” because of its maker's nationality. Conversely, do not assume every cross-cultural combination is invalid: investigate authorship and context rather than prescribing purity.

Contemporary material aesthetics: research notes

Contemporary material aesthetics: research notes

Research date: 7 September 2026. Scope: Claymorphism, Organic Tech, Dirty Gradients, Spray, and Holographic Iridescence. This is an exploratory study, not a Levarum direction approval or a claim that any style improves conversion.

Evidence and terminology

I visually inspected the supplied, unmodified screenshots 02-claymorphism.jpeg, 03-organic-tech.jpeg, 04-dirty-gradients.jpeg, 07-spray.jpeg, and 09-overview.jpeg. Their trend labels are useful conversational handles, not mutually exclusive technical classifications. An object can be geometrically soft, iridescent in material, and surrounded by a grainy gradient simultaneously. The studios cited below do not necessarily classify their work under these labels.

I opened the primary pages linked below and read the retrieved content. I have not independently run their interactions or tested audience responses. Some separate embedded image requests failed; visual observations of the supplied screenshots are distinguished from creators' descriptions. The image showing “Simplicity21” led to a matching project title and named creators on an original portfolio; this supports project identification, but I have not performed a pixel-for-pixel match of its frame. Attribution of the other supplied crops remains unconfirmed.

1. Claymorphism: form and touch

Observed in the reference: the Simplicity21 crop contains oversized blue curved fins, coral cylinders, green folds, and orange rounded forms. Highlights and shadows communicate volume; softened edges and saturated colors create a tactile, toy-like impression. It is more varied than “pastel puffy UI cards.” Glossy and matte-looking surfaces coexist.

Documented original project: Kyungtae Kim and Toss Design's Simplicity 21 portfolio credits Kim with art direction and 3D design for Viva Republica's conference. Five objects represent the conference title and four themes: Obsession, Details, Extra Mile, and Paradigm Shift. The imagery has a content system behind it; it is not simply a collection of fashionable blobs.

Related primary case: Microsoft's Fluent emoji account describes building expressive characters from simple geometry, choosing imperfect silhouettes, and preserving emotional intent. It documents a communication purpose for playful volume. It does not establish that clay-styled marketing pages outperform other pages.

Interpretation: tactile form can make abstract concepts approachable and group related ideas through consistent material and lighting. In a creative conference, exuberance can feel appropriate; on a critical transaction screen the same scale and density could distract. A rendered object used as illustration does not automatically have the affordances of an actual button. Keep functional controls visually and semantically clear.

2. Organic Tech: biological complexity against rational structure

Observed: the supplied “Decode yourself” composition surrounds a very plain white information card with porous, tufted and mesh-like purple/pink volumes. The effect comes from contrast: intricate material outside, sparse typographic information inside. “Organic” here does not require leaves, green, or literal environmental messaging.

Documented adjacent case: Cartier's Farinart identity deliberately combines innovation and agricultural origins. Its designers describe modeled 3D grains, landscape photography, geometric iconography and contrasting type. The visual subject is connected to the company's actual material, rather than borrowing generic nature symbolism.

Interpretation: this family can express adaptation, interdependence, emergence, or a connection between living systems and engineered systems. Scale changes the reading: a small fuzzy object may seem friendly; an enlarged porous surface can suggest microscopy, biotech, fungi or something unsettling. Clean technical typography can make those textures feel examined and purposeful. Without a relevant subject or explanation, the same imagery can imply a scientific capability that the business does not possess. Nature-like appearance is also not evidence of environmental benefit.

3. Dirty Gradients: atmosphere with irregularity

Observed: the supplied six-poster crop uses broad horizontal transitions among blue, cream, orange, dark gray and ochre, with condensed information and looser script. The restrained layouts matter as much as the color. At this screenshot resolution, softness and muted mixing are visible; the precise grain-production method is not verifiable.

Working description: “dirty” can mean intentional noise, uneven density, desaturation, unexpected intermediate colors, imperfect boundaries or a print-like finish. Those are different choices. Adding random noise to a normal gradient does not by itself produce an authored identity.

Documented adjacent case: Pentagram's Intrinsic uses gradient echoes and particles to represent possible future robot positions. A generative tool provides behavioral parameters tied to the software; restrained technical typography and flat color provide a counterweight. This is not documented as a “Dirty Gradients” project, but it demonstrates how gradient behavior can explain something.

Interpretation: a field can suggest dawn, heat, depth or energy, while irregularity prevents a mathematically pristine surface from dominating the mood. Meaning depends on adjacent content: the same amber haze may read as sunset beside travel imagery and as temperature beside an engineering diagram. Legibility must be evaluated across the actual varying background, including mobile crops. A grain treatment may also disappear or become harsh after compression; the export requires inspection.

4. Spray: gesture, authorship and context

Observed: the supplied white coffee package uses vivid blue loose lettering, mountain/animal-like drawings and visibly soft sprayed edges. Small black product information provides a separate reading layer. The large marks feel immediate because they preserve the variability of a gesture, not because the entire package lacks order.

Documented contextual counterexample: Pentagram's American Weed Co. draws on military markings, including spray-painted stencils, after discovery with founders and veterans. This is a different use of spray vocabulary from the supplied playful coffee package. It demonstrates that production resemblance alone does not establish cultural meaning.

Interpretation: spray can suggest informal authorship, movement, street culture, workshop markings or protest, depending on the lettering, palette, imagery and product. It can make an otherwise precise layout feel alive. But genuine gesture is a designed asset, not permission to make critical text hard to read. One useful combination is expressive marks for emphasis with precise typesetting for facts. Avoid assuming the coffee package's original artist from a repost account or readable product name.

5. Holographic Iridescence: light-dependent material

Observed: the overview's small tile shows metallic-looking gray forms with shifting pastel/rainbow highlights. It is sufficient to discuss a visual impression, not identify the object, maker or physical process. “Holographic” is often used loosely in aesthetic conversation; a multicolor sheen is not evidence of an actual hologram.

Documented manufacturer reference: 3M's Dichroic DF Chill changes apparent color with viewing angle, and has different transmitted and reflected color ranges. This offers a real material reference for thinking about a simulated surface rather than merely placing a rainbow gradient everywhere.

Interpretation: carefully controlled highlights can communicate a responsive, unusual or precious material. Broad saturated rainbow sheen can instead suggest novelty packaging or club culture. Geometry, lighting direction, background and scale determine the effect together. A digital imitation should maintain a coherent light/material story; a flat multicolor fill and a reflective object do different jobs. Motion is optional: a well-chosen still can communicate the material without constant animation.

Combining styles by assigning jobs

An effective exploratory system can assign structure to typography/grid, material to one family of objects, color to a controlled gradient vocabulary, and detail to a limited gesture or finish. This is a design heuristic, not a universal formula. For example, organic geometry with one iridescent material may be coherent; combining clay softness, chrome, fur, spray, and multiple gradient directions on every component is harder to read because each element demands attention.

Play Studio's Adobe MAX case is a useful primary example of controlled variety. Its account describes the Adobe lens as an organizing device across imagery and motion, with core brand colors and varied secondary artwork. It also openly describes human-directed collage with generative tools. The transferable lesson is to inspect the organizing decisions and specificity of the work, rather than infer quality or provenance from an effect alone.

What the evidence cannot prove

These sources establish authorship, design intent and mechanisms; they do not establish a universal style ranking. A popular reel selects memorable pictures under conditions different from finding information or booking a service. Studio case studies are also curated accounts; favorable client comments cannot isolate the effect of one visual technique. A design may be famous because of the underlying brand, distribution or novelty.

Useful next research questions are therefore concrete: what should a visitor understand, which element creates that understanding, how does it behave at a small size, and what remains when motion or decoration is absent? Comparable specimens and audience comprehension checks can investigate those questions. No Levarum logo, palette, typography or website implementation is approved by this research.

Perception, material and motion: a design learning note

Perception, material and motion: a design learning note

Prepared 7 September 2026. This is a learning resource, not a proposed website or brand identity. Inspected the supplied Organic Tech, Claymorphism, Dirty Gradients, Clean Girl Brutalism and overview JPEGs in ../references/. They are low-resolution social-media captures with interface overlays, not source artwork or working websites. The style names are the video's labels, not universally agreed categories. Holographic Iridescence appears only as a small overview thumbnail.

Observed: Organic Tech places a clean white “Decode yourself” panel over porous purple and pink forms. Claymorphism combines brightly colored folded, rounded and glossy shapes behind white event text. Dirty Gradients shows six poster rectangles with broad horizontal color transitions and mixed type. Clean Girl Brutalism places a very heavy “AGNI” heading above a large quiet area and small pink information block. The holographic thumbnail suggests reflective, multicolored packaging, but is too small to identify its material or technique. These observations motivate the principles below; they do not verify readability or interaction.

What makes these styles work

Heavy editorial type and space. Large letters can become the dominant shape of a composition: the reader encounters a statement before inspecting its supporting details. But hierarchy is relational. If the headline, navigation, caption and button all demand equal emphasis, increasing their weight does not establish a useful reading order. My design interpretation is to allocate one dominant typographic gesture, then support it with quieter, sufficiently legible information. Negative space is the interval that keeps those roles distinct; it can be colored or textured rather than literally white.

Cooper Hewitt's *How Posters Work* frames posters as deliberate experiments in perception and storytelling. Its account of Philippe Apeloig's *Vivo in Typo* documents typography constructed through punctuation, scale, spacing, drawing and print texture. These are useful precedents for studying how type can serve as image; neither source establishes that a particular font improves website conversion. Museum exhibition; documented making process.

Organic tech and clay-like forms. Rounded contours, broad soft highlights and grounding shadows can suggest volume and softness. “Friendly,” “futuristic” and “expensive” are interpretations, not physical properties or universal reactions. A shape with attractive rendering can still be irrelevant to the message. Ask what its form explains: a connection, transformation, sequence, container, or simply atmosphere. If it is atmosphere, do not give it the strongest visual emphasis by default.

Material appearance is not determined by a single color. Storrs, Anderson and Fleming's research examines the interaction of reflectance, lighting and geometry in perceived gloss, including misperceptions. That supports caution about simplistic recipes such as “add a highlight to make metal.” It does not prove a particular clay illustration feels reassuring or authentic. Open research paper.

Grainy or “dirty” gradients. Here the useful distinction is between a slow change in color and small-scale surface variation. My interpretation is that grain can interrupt the perfectly smooth appearance associated with digital rendering and suggest print, pigment or photographic texture. Its effect depends on density, scale and placement: texture that is appealing in an empty field can interfere with thin text. The lesson is not “grain equals craft.” Craft includes choosing where the texture stops and protecting the reading surface. Apeloig's process is a relevant precedent for purposeful tactility, not evidence that digital noise reproduces screenprinting.

Holographic or iridescent treatments. In interface design these labels often describe a visual simulation: shifting bands of color, sharp highlights and alternating light/dark regions. A static multicolor gradient alone does not establish physical iridescence. For learning, distinguish hue variation from highlight structure and apparent depth. My interpretation is that the material effect becomes more convincing when color and lighting appear to belong to a coherent surface. The practical consequence is to separate decorative material rendering from text and control states. A button's identity should survive when the decorative sheen is removed.

Grouping makes space meaningful

Gestalt principles offer explanations of relationships, not an automatic template. Palmer describes grouping by proximity, similarity, common region, connectedness and synchrony. A bounded field can make separated items read as one set; changes at the same time can also imply a relationship. Researcher's own discussion.

Applied interpretation: before adding boxes to every item, first adjust their relative distances. Put a caption visibly closer to its image than to the next image. Conversely, if independent actions sit inside one prominent organic shape, that shared region may imply they are one action. A decorative line joining unrelated elements can communicate a relationship the copy does not support. Motion has the same responsibility: synchronized movement can imply a common system even when the elements are independent. On a narrow screen, preserve the intended relationships when the layout changes; merely stacking everything can produce a new grouping.

Translate the principle, not the poster dimensions

A poster has a fixed surface. A website must accommodate longer text, different viewport widths, zoom and interaction. Preserve its hierarchy, rhythm and material character while letting the composition change. A dramatic headline can wrap differently on a phone; a peripheral sculpture can become a smaller detail; a full-page texture can become an isolated image. These are design translations rather than evidence of aesthetic compromise.

Use editable text for meaningful headings and descriptions so it can reflow. W3C's Reflow criterion expects ordinary vertically scrolling content to work at a width equivalent to 320 CSS pixels without lost information or functionality or two-dimensional scrolling, subject to specified exceptions. That is a testable requirement, not proof that every single-column composition is usable. W3C Reflow explanation.

For gradients, contrast must be considered where the letters actually sit, including changing backgrounds. W3C specifies 4.5:1 for ordinary text and 3:1 for qualifying large text, with exceptions. Thin or unusual fonts can remain visually faint despite nominally passing values. My recommendation is a controlled reading region rather than trusting a palette swatch sampled elsewhere. No screenshot in this study has received a contrast pass. W3C Contrast explanation.

Motion can demonstrate a transformation or acknowledge an action, but a living background does not need to make reading itself move. W3C's Animation from Interactions criterion is Level AAA and addresses disabling nonessential interaction-triggered motion. Separately, Level A Pause, Stop, Hide covers qualifying automatically moving information lasting more than five seconds alongside other content. Reduced-motion support and a pause control answer related but different needs. Interaction motion; automatic movement.

Rendering cost depends on implementation. Google's browser guidance recommends favoring transform and opacity where appropriate, and warns against indiscriminate will-change. This is not a guarantee that a large blurred animated layer will be cheap. For these material studies, compare a static rendered texture with the animated version on actual devices before accepting the added work. Record asset bytes and a performance trace, rather than declaring a design fast from its appearance. Browser performance guidance.

Three small teaching experiments

These are proposed experiments, not completed tests. All can run in one local HTML file with CSS, inline SVG and JavaScript, native labeled controls, and no network requests.

  1. Space establishes relationships. Keep a heading, two image placeholders, two captions and a button identical. A range input changes only the gap between each image and its caption; maintain the outer canvas size. Ask which caption belongs to which image and what feels grouped. Show the current gap numerically and provide reset. Compare the same setting at wide and narrow widths separately. This demonstrates a spacing effect without simultaneously changing font or color.
  1. Texture versus reading. Keep layout, type, text color and base gradient fixed. A slider changes only the opacity of one deterministic grain layer. Ask when the surface becomes more tactile and when small text becomes harder to inspect. Keep the grain seed fixed so settings are comparable. Label subjective responses as preferences; do not output an invented accessibility score. A separate solid-background reference helps explain why a palette's nominal contrast cannot describe every textured pixel.
  1. Still versus moving material. Use an identical decorative form with a fixed iridescent-looking surface. Compare a still state with the same form translating through a small distance; do not also rotate, resize or recolor it. A user-activated Play/Pause button controls movement, and reduced-motion preference defaults it to still. Ask whether the motion explains anything or competes with the unchanged text. Keep the scene visible when paused. The experiment isolates motion; it does not claim to simulate optical iridescence accurately.

Unknowns include the original references' authorship and production techniques, actual target fonts and assets, motion behavior, device performance, and audience comprehension. Learning guide and how to use this library

Visual design study — a learning library

Start with relationships, then learn the names

The first lesson is that a design gets much of its character from contrast between elements. In the Organic Tech reference, complex porous forms surround a plain information panel. In the AGNI example, a very heavy heading sits above a large quiet field and a compact pink block. In the Spray example, expressive blue marks coexist with precise black product information. These are observations of the supplied images. They explain a composition more specifically than saying it is modern or premium.

Read a reference through five questions:

  1. What attracts attention first: scale, contrast, color, isolation, or detail?
  2. Which items appear related, and what makes them belong together?
  3. What creates the apparent material: edge, lighting, texture, opacity, or reflection?
  4. What is doing the informational work, and what is providing atmosphere?
  5. What would remain if the decorative effects were removed?

These questions are our teaching method, not a scientifically validated scoring rubric. We will use them to describe what we see before deciding whether we like it.

The ten labels do not name the same kind of thing

  • Risograph: a documented printing technology with separations, spot inks, halftones and registration. A digital grain effect is only an analogy.
  • Claymorphism: a useful informal label for soft dimensional form. The supplied example also includes vivid folds and glossy objects; it is not simply pastel puffy cards.
  • Organic Tech: a descriptive pairing of biological-looking material and rational information design. The screenshot does not establish an author or a formal movement.
  • Holographic Iridescence: the overview suggests multicolored reflective material. A static rainbow gradient does not establish holography. Dichroic and iridescent effects should be studied through real material behavior.
  • Dirty Gradients: a working description of irregular, hazy or textured color transitions, not one verified production recipe.
  • Austurbane: a contemporary aesthetic classification that CARI attributes to Alex Edwards. It is not an old architectural school or a universally adopted term.
  • Asian Chic: the phrase has documented usage in fashion scholarship, but the small graphic thumbnail cannot establish a single cultural lineage or graphic-design movement.
  • Clean Girl Brutalism: retain the source's name as a label. Its precise origin and stable definition were not established. Analyze the AGNI example's scale, space and information placement directly.
  • Spray: a family of gestural or stencil-like visual techniques. Meaning depends on context, lettering and authorship.
  • Gzhel: a regional ceramic tradition. The supplied LOEWE bag is not authenticated as Gzhel; a possible De Morgan connection remains a research lead, not a correction presented as fact.

The sources and qualifications for these classifications are in the three research chapters. The original social-media post and most pictured artwork credits remain unresolved. A repost account is not an artwork credit.

Three experiments before choosing any direction

1. Change the surface; hold the arrangement still

The material demonstration uses exactly the same two shapes and positions in every state. Flat color emphasizes silhouette. Layered ink introduces overlap and grain. Soft-volume shading adds a coordinated light direction. Haze dissolves boundaries. A multicolor band suggests an iridescent surface. These compare bundled material cues such as shading plus shadows; they are not single-variable causal tests, authentic print samples or physically accurate rendering.

Ask: which treatment makes you inspect the shapes, which makes you read the empty space, and which dominates the whole composition? There is no correct preference. Naming the cause of a preference gives us something useful to develop later.

2. Change spacing; watch relationships change

In the spacing exercise, the outer content and type stay fixed while the gap between a title and its description changes. The purpose is to notice grouping, not to discover one ideal pixel value. Test the same gap on a wide and a narrow view. Context changes what feels connected.

3. Change texture; watch the reading surface

In the texture exercise, a deterministic grain layer varies in strength over a fixed gradient and fixed text. Look for the point where texture draws attention away from the words. This is a visual preference experiment; it does not calculate a WCAG result or prove readability for other people.

The motion experiment proposed in the perception chapter remains a later lesson. The three implemented exercises in this edition are material, spacing and texture.

Brainstorming without committing to an identity

We can study combinations by giving each technique a job. Typography and spacing provide structure; an object supplies form and material; a color field supplies atmosphere; a small gesture supplies emphasis. This is a design heuristic. It is useful because the effects do not all have to compete for the same role.

Four independent questions for later studies:

  • Can a very precise typographic composition remain lively with just one irregular printed layer?
  • Can one soft dimensional object explain a theme clearly enough that the same idea works in a flat silhouette?
  • Can a rich gradient remain expressive after its colors are reduced to two and its motion is removed?
  • Can a repeated motif create a family across a cover, diagram and small icon without becoming a wallpaper over everything?

These are open experiments. They do not select a palette, typeface, symbol, visual direction, or implementation for Levarum.

Learning sequence

First, learn to see hierarchy and relationships. Second, study how printing, lighting and texture create material cues. Third, connect effects to the content and context of original projects. Fourth, study motion, mobile composition and reading comfort. Fifth, compare controlled combinations with the same content. Only after those lessons should we discuss a particular Levarum direction.
